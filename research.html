Research

Understanding the influences that shape the use of physical activity spaces.

My research examines how social, environmental, historical, and structural conditions influence opportunities to engage with physical activity spaces in historically marginalized communities and how communities experience these spaces designed to support movement, recreation, and health.

Doctoral Research / Research Focus

Factors Influencing Use of Physical Activity Spaces in Formerly Redlined Neighborhoods

When I began my Doctor of Education (EdD) in Kinesiology, I knew I wanted my research to contribute to understanding and ultimately mitigating the barriers that historically marginalized communities face in accessing physical activity and recreational opportunities.

During my academic journey, I encountered the concept of physical activity deserts, an idea similar to food deserts. At its core, the concept recognizes that opportunities to engage in physical activity are not equally accessible across communities and that these disparities can have significant implications for health, well-being, and quality of life.

Much of the existing research surrounding physical activity deserts focuses on geographic proximity and the availability of recreational infrastructure. However, having grown up in the San Francisco Bay Area, I believed there was more to the story. I recognized that physical activity deserts can exist even in communities where parks, recreation centers, and other physical activity spaces are physically present.

What makes a physical activity space truly accessible?

Simply having a park or recreational facility nearby does not necessarily mean that residents feel welcome, safe, comfortable, or able to use it. I became interested in understanding the social, environmental, economic, and structural factors that influence whether individuals engage with the recreational infrastructure available in their neighborhoods.

Historical Context / Redlining

Understanding the legacy of place

I also wanted to connect these questions to public policy, public health, and the historical forces that have shaped neighborhood development. Specifically, I became interested in redlining, a discriminatory housing and lending practice associated with the historical grading of neighborhoods by the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) and broader patterns of racial discrimination and disinvestment.

To what extent might the legacy of historical redlining or discriminatory public policy continue to influence how residents experience and utilize physical activity spaces today?

My research brings these interests together by examining how social, environmental, historical, and structural conditions influence opportunities to engage in physical activity within formerly redlined communities. More importantly, it seeks to understand how residents perceive and experience spaces intended to support movement, recreation, and health.

Ultimately, I hope this work contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of physical activity access—one that moves beyond the mere presence or proximity of infrastructure and recognizes the importance of the lived experiences of the communities these spaces are intended to serve.

Why This Research Matters

Communities can appear well served when access is measured only by the presence of parks, recreation facilities, trails, courts, or other physical activity infrastructure. That measure can overlook whether those resources are actually usable or meaningful to residents.

A more complete understanding of access can help practitioners, researchers, planners, recreation agencies, and policymakers identify barriers that traditional spatial measures may miss.

  • Improve how physical activity access is evaluated.
  • Better incorporate community experience into planning and program development.
  • Identify barriers related to safety, quality, infrastructure, transportation, and perception.
  • Support more equitable investment in recreation and physical activity environments.
  • Connect research findings to practical decisions within communities and public agencies.

Research should inform better environments and better decisions.

My goal is to connect scholarship with practical applications in physical activity, recreation, community programming, planning, and organizational decision-making.
