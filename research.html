Research

Understanding the environments that shape physical activity.

My research examines how social, environmental, historical, and structural conditions influence opportunities to engage in physical activity and how communities experience the spaces designed to support movement, recreation, and health.

Research Focus

My work is situated at the intersection of kinesiology, physical activity, community environments, social determinants of health, and environmental justice.

I am particularly interested in understanding why physical activity opportunities may exist spatially but remain functionally inaccessible, undesirable, unsafe, underutilized, or disconnected from the communities they are intended to serve.

This perspective moves beyond simply asking whether parks, recreation centers, trails, courts, or other resources are present. Instead, my research examines how history, perception, infrastructure, social conditions, quality, safety, transportation, and lived experience influence whether those spaces become meaningful opportunities for participation.

Doctoral Research

Redlining & the Creation of Functional Physical Activity Deserts

My doctoral research explores how historical patterns of neighborhood disinvestment and exclusion may continue to influence physical activity environments and experiences within communities today.

The study focuses not only on spatial access to physical activity resources, but also on what influences whether residents actually view those spaces as usable, accessible, welcoming, safe, and relevant to their lives.

By combining community perspectives with environmental and spatial analysis, the research seeks to develop a more complete understanding of what I describe as functional physical activity access: the difference between having a resource nearby and having a realistic opportunity to use it.

Core Research Themes

Several interconnected themes guide the questions I ask and the way I approach physical activity environments.

Spatial Access

Where physical activity resources are located and how geography, distance, transportation, and the built environment influence access.

Functional Access

Whether available spaces can realistically be used based on quality, safety, maintenance, social context, cost, programming, and community experience.

Historical Context

How historic housing, planning, investment, and neighborhood policies may continue to shape modern physical activity environments.

Lived Experience

How residents perceive, navigate, value, avoid, and engage with physical activity spaces within their communities.

Research Methods

My research uses multiple forms of evidence to connect community experience with environmental and spatial context.

01

Survey Research

Collecting quantitative and descriptive information about physical activity behavior, barriers, perceptions, environments, and community experience.

02

Interviews

Using semi-structured interviews to explore lived experience and understand the factors that may not be visible through spatial or quantitative data alone.

03

GIS & Spatial Analysis

Mapping physical activity resources and examining their relationship to historic and contemporary neighborhood conditions.

04

Direct Observation

Examining the physical characteristics, condition, usability, accessibility, and surrounding environment of physical activity spaces.

Conceptual Frameworks

My research draws from frameworks that recognize physical activity as the product of interacting individual, environmental, social, and structural influences.

Socioecological Perspective

Physical activity is influenced by factors operating across multiple levels, including individual experience, social relationships, organizations, neighborhoods, policies, and the broader environment.

Social Determinants of Health

Health and physical activity opportunities are shaped by the conditions in which people live, learn, work, move, and participate in their communities.

Environmental Justice

The distribution, quality, accessibility, and experience of community resources can reflect broader histories of investment, exclusion, and inequity.

Ecological Systems Thinking

Individual experiences occur within interconnected systems, making it important to examine how local environments interact with institutions, policies, histories, and social conditions.

Why This Research Matters

Communities can appear well served when access is measured only by the presence of parks, recreation facilities, trails, courts, or other physical activity resources. That measure can overlook whether those resources are actually usable or meaningful to residents.

A more complete understanding of access can help practitioners, researchers, planners, recreation agencies, and policymakers identify barriers that traditional spatial measures may miss.

  • Improve how physical activity access is evaluated.
  • Better incorporate community experience into planning and program development.
  • Identify barriers related to safety, quality, infrastructure, transportation, and perception.
  • Support more equitable investment in recreation and physical activity environments.
  • Connect research findings to practical decisions within communities and public agencies.
